# Benzodiazepine Deprescribing in Older Adults: Digital Prompts, Pharmacist Interventions, and Shared Decisions

> **Editorial status:** Evidence-based editorial draft. Independent clinical and pharmacy review is required before publication. Evidence cutoff: **2026-08-18**. This page is educational and does not provide an individualized dose schedule.

## Key answer

Education, pharmacist-led review, and clinician decision support can reduce benzodiazepine use in older adults, but successful implementation depends on alternatives, follow-up, and avoidance of abrupt stopping. The practical interpretation must remain within the population, duration, formulation, and outcomes that were actually studied. [1–2]

A safe interpretation starts with the exact medication or intervention, the reason it is being used, the formulation, treatment duration, other medicines, current symptoms, and the risks of both continuing and changing treatment. The article therefore explains decision factors and safety boundaries rather than prescribing a fixed schedule.

This draft prioritizes regulator-hosted product information, current clinical guidance, systematic reviews, randomized trials, and carefully labeled observational or mechanistic evidence. The references below are used according to their design and limitations [1–2]. Absence of a detected signal is not rewritten as absence of risk, and a population average is not presented as an individual prediction.

## The finding in one sentence

Education, pharmacist-led review, and clinician decision support can reduce benzodiazepine use in older adults, but successful implementation depends on alternatives, follow-up, and avoidance of abrupt stopping.

## Why this question matters

Population-level prescribing alerts can improve review but can also create forced tapering if not paired with patient-centered clinical capacity. Implementation evidence is as important as pharmacology.

## What evidence was reviewed

Studies include mailed patient education, pharmacist outreach, electronic prompts, academic detailing, medication review, and shared-decision interventions. Outcomes include initiation of tapering, dose reduction, discontinuation, falls, sleep, and patient experience.

## What the studies found

Many interventions increase conversations and reduce prescribing or use. Effects differ by clinician engagement, access to CBT-I and alternatives, patient readiness, and follow-up duration. Simple alerts are less effective when they create work without a supported pathway.

## Key limitations and sources of uncertainty

Discontinuation is often the primary outcome even when sleep, anxiety, falls, or quality of life are not fully measured. Participants may be healthier and more motivated than the general older population. Health-system context limits transportability.

## What this may change in practice

Use prompts to trigger review, not automatic dose reduction. Include a pharmacist, a patient education tool, alternatives, monitoring, and a documented exception process. Measure harm and function as well as reduced use.

## What the evidence does not justify

Evidence does not justify blanket age cutoffs, punitive prescribing metrics, or replacing benzodiazepines with antipsychotics, anticholinergics, or other risky sedatives.

## What research should do next

Implementation trials should include frailty, dementia, rural access, caregiver roles, equity, falls, cognition, and sustainability after prompts are removed.

## Funding and conflict-of-interest note

Drug-development studies are often sponsored by manufacturers, while guideline panels and independent reviews can also include authors with industry relationships. Publication should list the funding source and declared conflicts for every pivotal study. A declared conflict does not invalidate a result, but it raises the importance of protocol registration, complete outcome reporting, independent replication, and separation of evidence from promotional language.

## When urgent medical assessment may be needed

        Seek urgent local medical assessment when any of the following applies. This list is intentionally conservative and is not a diagnostic checklist:

        - A trial result is described as proof for populations, durations, doses, or combinations that were not studied
- Absence of a detected signal is rewritten as a categorical absence-of-risk claim
- A network meta-analysis is presented as a head-to-head randomized comparison
- Regulatory approval in one country is described as global approval

        Emergency pathways and telephone numbers vary by country. A tracking application or educational article cannot rule out an emergency.

## Questions to take to the prescriber

- Does this evidence apply to my diagnosis, age, treatment duration, formulation, and other medications?
- Was the finding based on a randomized comparison, an indirect comparison, an observational study, or mechanistic modeling?
- Were withdrawal, relapse, function, serious adverse events, and long-term outcomes measured directly?
- What was not studied, and what uncertainty remains for my situation?
- Would this finding actually change a clinical decision, or only support further research?

## Frequently asked questions

### Do patient letters work?

They can prompt successful conversations for some people, but require access to clinical support and alternatives.

### Should a pharmacist lead the taper?

Pharmacists can improve reconciliation and formulation planning within a coordinated prescriber-led pathway.

### What is a bad metric?

Counting discontinuation alone without monitoring sleep, function, withdrawal, or substitution harms.

### How should an app prompt users?

Invite a medication review and prepare questions, rather than declare the medicine inappropriate.

## Regional and formulation note

Approval status, labels, and clinical practice vary by country. Verify the exact product label, manufacturer, strength, release system, and local clinical pathway before publication or use. A tablet or capsule instruction that is correct for one product may be wrong for another.
