# Benzodiazepines in Older Adults: Falls, Cognition, Polypharmacy, and Tapering Decisions

> **Editorial status:** Specialist-review evidence draft. Do not publish until the named specialist and a clinical pharmacist have approved every medical and formulation claim. Evidence cutoff: **2026-08-18**. This page is educational and does not provide an individualized dose schedule.

## Key answer

How to balance fall, cognitive, interaction, and withdrawal risks when considering benzodiazepine reduction in older adults. The central safety principle is: Falls, fractures, delirium, cognitive effects, driving impairment, sedation, and interactions. [1–4]

A safe interpretation starts with the exact medication or intervention, the reason it is being used, the formulation, treatment duration, other medicines, current symptoms, and the risks of both continuing and changing treatment. The article therefore explains decision factors and safety boundaries rather than prescribing a fixed schedule.

## At a glance

| Question or decision | Evidence-based answer |
|---|---|
| Reasons to review | Falls, fractures, delirium, cognitive effects, driving impairment, sedation, and interactions. |
| Reasons not to rush | Severe withdrawal, destabilized anxiety or sleep, seizure risk, and loss of function. |
| High-risk combinations | Opioids, gabapentinoids, alcohol, sedating antihistamines, antipsychotics, and other central nervous system depressants. |
| Best process | Shared decision-making, medication reconciliation, gradual individualized change, and fall and cognition monitoring. |

This draft prioritizes regulator-hosted product information, current clinical guidance, systematic reviews, randomized trials, and carefully labeled observational or mechanistic evidence. The references below are used according to their design and limitations [1–4]. Absence of a detected signal is not rewritten as absence of risk, and a population average is not presented as an individual prediction.

## Working definitions

| Term | Meaning in this article |
|---|---|
| Withdrawal symptoms | New or intensified symptoms after a dose reduction, missed dose, formulation change, or stop when physiologic adaptation is a plausible contributor. |
| Rebound | Temporary return of a treated symptom above its pretreatment baseline. |
| Relapse or recurrence | Return of the underlying treated condition. |
| Physical dependence | Physiologic adaptation; it is not the same as addiction or a substance use disorder. |

## Why age changes medication risk

Older adults may have slower clearance, greater brain sensitivity, frailty, impaired balance, sleep apnea, kidney or liver impairment, and more interacting medicines. A dose tolerated years ago may become unsafe after illness, weight change, or a new prescription.

## Why Beers Criteria is a prompt, not an automatic stop order

The Beers Criteria identifies potentially inappropriate medication use at the population level. It supports review, alternatives, and risk reduction. It does not justify abrupt discontinuation or ignore a valid indication, prior failed reductions, or individual goals.

## Assess falls and cognition before changing the dose

Document falls, near-falls, orthostatic symptoms, gait, driving, daytime sleepiness, delirium history, memory concerns, vision, alcohol, and nighttime bathroom trips. A baseline helps determine whether reduction improves function or causes destabilization.

## Review the whole sedative burden

Risk often comes from combinations rather than one medication. Opioids, gabapentinoids, Z-drugs, sedating antidepressants, anticholinergics, antihistamines, and alcohol can compound sedation and breathing risk. Sequence changes deliberately rather than removing several agents at once.

## Support sleep and anxiety without substitution cascades

CBT-I, pain care, hearing and vision support, daytime activity, treatment of sleep apnea, and psychotherapy may help. Replacing a benzodiazepine with an antipsychotic or strongly anticholinergic sedative can exchange one risk for another.

## When urgent medical assessment may be needed

        Seek urgent local medical assessment when any of the following applies. This list is intentionally conservative and is not a diagnostic checklist:

        - Recent falls, head injury, delirium, or major driving impairment
- Slow or difficult breathing with opioid or other sedative co-use
- Seizure or severe withdrawal symptoms
- The person cannot manage a complex taper regimen safely without support

        Emergency pathways and telephone numbers vary by country. A tracking application or educational article cannot rule out an emergency.

## Questions to take to the prescriber

- What is the current indication and treatment goal?
- Which alternative explanations for the symptoms need assessment?
- What is the exact medication, formulation, timing, and most recent change?
- Which outcome should be monitored before another change is considered?
- What are the urgent warning signs and the fastest route back to the clinical team?

## Frequently asked questions

### Should every adult over 65 stop benzodiazepines?

No. Use should be reviewed, but individual indication, benefit, risk, dependence, and alternatives matter.

### Does dementia mean tapering will improve cognition?

Not necessarily. Sedation may improve, but cognitive impairment has many causes and withdrawal can temporarily worsen function.

### Is diazepam substitution best in older adults?

Not automatically. Its long half-life and active metabolites can accumulate and increase sedation.

### Who should be involved?

The patient, caregiver when appropriate, prescriber, pharmacist, and relevant geriatric, sleep, psychiatric, or neurologic clinicians.

## Regional and formulation note

Guidelines, labels, formulations, and care pathways vary by country. Verify the exact product label, manufacturer, strength, release system, and local clinical pathway before publication or use. A tablet or capsule instruction that is correct for one product may be wrong for another.
