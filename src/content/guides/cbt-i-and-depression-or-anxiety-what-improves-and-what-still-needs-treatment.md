# CBT-I With Depression or Anxiety: What May Improve and What Still Needs Treatment

> **Editorial status:** Specialist-review evidence draft. CBT-I is a structured treatment, not a substitute for urgent assessment or treatment of another sleep, medical, or psychiatric disorder.

## Key answer

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (**CBT-I**) is the recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia. It combines assessment, stimulus control, sleep scheduling or sleep-restriction methods, cognitive strategies, and relapse prevention. The treatment can be delivered in person, by telehealth, in groups, or through validated digital programs, but the components and level of supervision may need adaptation when another condition changes the safety of sleep loss or the interpretation of symptoms. [1–3]

## At a glance

| Question | Evidence-based answer |
|---|---|
| Is CBT-I the same as sleep hygiene? | No. Sleep hygiene is supportive education; multicomponent CBT-I is an active treatment. [1] |
| Does it work with comorbid illness? | Often yes, and guidelines recommend it across many comorbidities, but parallel illnesses still need parallel care. [2,3] |
| Can it be digital? | Validated digital CBT-I can be effective; therapist support, engagement, and complexity influence results. |
| Is sleep restriction always used unchanged? | No. Time-in-bed reduction may need adaptation for bipolar disorder, seizure risk, excessive sleepiness, falls, pregnancy, or unstable illness. [3] |
| Does CBT-I guarantee medication discontinuation or cure another condition? | No. It treats insomnia mechanisms and may support other goals, but it does not erase medication-specific risk or replace other treatment. |

## What CBT-I includes

A full program usually begins with a sleep and medical history plus a sleep diary. **Stimulus control** strengthens the connection between bed and sleep. **Sleep scheduling** or sleep restriction therapy reduces excessive time awake in bed and later expands the sleep window as sleep consolidates. **Cognitive strategies** test catastrophic predictions and unhelpful monitoring. Relapse-prevention planning prepares for travel, illness, stress, medication changes, or symptom flares. Relaxation and sleep-hygiene education may support the program but do not replace the core treatment. [1–3]


## What may improve together

Insomnia, depression, and anxiety can reinforce one another through arousal, rumination, irregular sleep, reduced activity, and anticipatory fear about the next night. Treating insomnia often improves sleep and can also reduce depressive or anxiety symptoms on average. A 2026 meta-analysis of 16 trials of unguided digital CBT-I in people with comorbid depression found improvements in both insomnia and depressive symptoms, but heterogeneity was very high. Older meta-analytic evidence also found smaller average improvements in anxiety and depression from internet CBT-I. [4,5]

## What CBT-I does not replace

CBT-I is not a complete treatment for major depression, generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, trauma-related disorders, psychosis, or suicidality. Mood and anxiety symptoms need their own assessment and evidence-based treatment. Improvement in sleep can make other care easier; lack of mood improvement does not mean the insomnia treatment failed.

## Interpreting early sleep changes

A person starting CBT-I may feel temporarily sleepier as time in bed is adjusted. That should not be confused automatically with worsening depression. Conversely, rapidly decreasing need for sleep, increased energy, pressured speech, impulsivity, or unusual confidence can suggest hypomania or mania and requires prompt assessment rather than continuing a standard self-guided sleep-restriction plan.

## Outcome table

| Outcome | What to expect from evidence |
|---|---|
| Insomnia severity | Consistent improvement on average. |
| Depressive symptoms | Often improve, but effects vary and do not replace depression treatment. |
| Anxiety symptoms | Small-to-moderate average improvement in some reviews; heterogeneous. |
| Suicidality | Important to assess directly; current evidence is not strong enough to use CBT-I as a sole suicide-prevention intervention. |


## Safety and adaptation

Transient sleepiness can occur when time in bed is compressed. A trained clinician should review driving, safety-sensitive work, falls, untreated sleep apnea, uncontrolled seizure disorder, bipolar disorder, active substance-use problems, acute suicidality or psychosis, pregnancy or postpartum demands, and unstable medical illness. The response may be a gentler sleep-compression approach, a wider initial sleep window, closer monitoring, treatment of another disorder first, or referral to a behavioral sleep medicine specialist. [3]

## What to track

Track bedtime, final wake time, estimated sleep onset, awakenings, naps, daytime sleepiness, functional safety, relevant symptoms, medication timing, alcohol or other sedatives, and adherence to the agreed components. Wearables can show trends but do not diagnose insomnia or sleep apnea and should not overrule a clinical assessment.

## Questions to take to the clinical team

- Has insomnia disorder been assessed, or could another sleep disorder explain the pattern?
- Which CBT-I components are appropriate now, and which need adaptation?
- What outcome will be monitored: insomnia severity, function, medication use, mood, pain, PAP use, or another goal?
- What symptoms require stopping the self-guided plan and contacting the team?
- Is the digital or group program validated, and is clinician support available if progress stalls?

## Frequently asked questions

### Can insomnia treatment prevent depression?

Some trials suggest reduced risk in selected populations, including a 2025 app-based trial in young people with insomnia and subclinical depression, but this is not a guarantee for every person.

### Should antidepressants be stopped when CBT-I begins?

No. Starting CBT-I does not by itself justify a medication change.

### What if anxiety rises during sleep scheduling?

Bring the diary and symptom timing to the clinician. The sleep window, cognitive work, and support level may need adaptation.

### When is urgent help needed?

Immediate self-harm risk, psychosis, mania, inability to care for oneself, or dangerous sleepiness requires urgent local assessment.
