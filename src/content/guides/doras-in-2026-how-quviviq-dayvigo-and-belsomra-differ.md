# DORAs in 2026: How Quviviq, Dayvigo, and Belsomra Differ

> **Editorial status:** Evidence-based editorial draft. Independent clinical and pharmacy review is required before publication. Evidence cutoff: **2026-08-18**. This page is educational and does not provide an individualized dose schedule.

## Key answer

Daridorexant (Quviviq), lemborexant (Dayvigo), and suvorexant (Belsomra) share dual orexin receptor antagonism but differ in approved doses, pharmacokinetics, trial programs, interaction language, and regional labeling; no network estimate makes one universally best. The practical interpretation must remain within the population, duration, formulation, and outcomes that were actually studied. [1–3]

A safe interpretation starts with the exact medication or intervention, the reason it is being used, the formulation, treatment duration, other medicines, current symptoms, and the risks of both continuing and changing treatment. The article therefore explains decision factors and safety boundaries rather than prescribing a fixed schedule.

## Evidence basis and uncertainty

This draft prioritizes regulator-hosted product information, current clinical guidance, systematic reviews, randomized trials, and carefully labeled observational or mechanistic evidence. The references below are used according to their design and limitations [1–3]. Absence of a detected signal is not rewritten as absence of risk, and a population average is not presented as an individual prediction.

| Reference | Evidence type | How it is used in this draft |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Regulator-hosted product labeling | Trial-based withdrawal and rebound assessments must not be rewritten as universal absence of risk. |
| 2 | Network meta-analysis | Indirect comparisons are not equivalent to head-to-head trials and must be interpreted with dose, duration, and outcome differences. |
| 3 | Clinical practice guideline | Strongly recommends multicomponent CBT-I for chronic insomnia; sleep hygiene alone is not equivalent to CBT-I. |

## The finding in one sentence

Daridorexant (Quviviq), lemborexant (Dayvigo), and suvorexant (Belsomra) share dual orexin receptor antagonism but differ in approved doses, pharmacokinetics, trial programs, interaction language, and regional labeling; no network estimate makes one universally best.

## Why this question matters

People often search by brand and encounter tables that imply a simple ranking. The clinically relevant choice also depends on sleep-onset versus maintenance symptoms, next-day function, age, liver function, respiratory disease, interacting CYP3A medicines, driving demands, cost, and country-specific approval. A comparison must use the generic name as the canonical entity and treat brands as search synonyms.

## What evidence was reviewed

The core evidence consists of regulator-hosted prescribing information, placebo-controlled insomnia trials, extension studies, and systematic reviews or network meta-analyses. Labels provide the most reliable product-specific details. Network meta-analysis can compare treatments indirectly across different trials, but differences in participants, outcome definitions, dose, timing, and study duration reduce certainty.

## What the studies found

All three medications improve selected sleep outcomes compared with placebo in approved populations. The magnitude differs by outcome and dose, and subjective and polysomnographic measures do not always move together. Their elimination profiles and next-day warnings differ, but a half-life table alone cannot predict morning impairment. Product labels also address complex sleep behaviors, driving, narcolepsy contraindication, CYP3A interactions, and use in respiratory or hepatic impairment.

## Key limitations and sources of uncertainty

There are few direct head-to-head randomized comparisons. Trials often exclude severe sleep apnea, unstable psychiatric illness, substance-use disorders, complex polypharmacy, pregnancy, and many frail patients. Most outcomes are short term compared with years of real-world use. Indirect rank probabilities can exaggerate small, uncertain differences and should not be converted into “best DORA” claims.

## What this may change in practice

Build a product comparison from current labels with columns for generic name, region, approved indication, dose range, CYP3A restrictions, half-life context, next-day precautions, and studied populations. Pair that with first-line CBT-I information. The final choice belongs to the prescriber and patient after reviewing comorbidities and goals.

## What the evidence does not justify

The evidence does not justify substituting one DORA for another milligram-for-milligram, promising no next-day impairment, declaring a class-wide absence of withdrawal, or recommending a DORA solely because it ranks first in an indirect analysis.

## What research should do next

Independent head-to-head trials should use patient-important outcomes such as daytime function, falls, driving, durable remission, and discontinuation effects. Longer pharmacovigilance and studies in cancer survivors, frailty, respiratory disease, and polypharmacy are priorities.

## Funding and conflict-of-interest note

Drug-development studies are often sponsored by manufacturers, while guideline panels and independent reviews can also include authors with industry relationships. Publication should list the funding source and declared conflicts for every pivotal study. A declared conflict does not invalidate a result, but it raises the importance of protocol registration, complete outcome reporting, independent replication, and separation of evidence from promotional language.

## When urgent medical assessment may be needed

        Seek urgent local medical assessment when any of the following applies. This list is intentionally conservative and is not a diagnostic checklist:

        - A trial result is described as proof for populations, durations, doses, or combinations that were not studied
- Absence of a detected signal is rewritten as a categorical absence-of-risk claim
- A network meta-analysis is presented as a head-to-head randomized comparison
- Regulatory approval in one country is described as global approval

        Emergency pathways and telephone numbers vary by country. A tracking application or educational article cannot rule out an emergency.

## Questions to take to the prescriber

- Does this evidence apply to my diagnosis, age, treatment duration, formulation, and other medications?
- Was the finding based on a randomized comparison, an indirect comparison, an observational study, or mechanistic modeling?
- Were withdrawal, relapse, function, serious adverse events, and long-term outcomes measured directly?
- What was not studied, and what uncertainty remains for my situation?
- Would this finding actually change a clinical decision, or only support further research?

## Frequently asked questions

### Which DORA has the shortest half-life?

Pharmacokinetic profiles differ, but half-life alone does not determine next-day function or the best treatment.

### Can the three medications be converted by dose?

No validated milligram-equivalence conversion should be assumed.

### Are DORAs first-line for chronic insomnia?

Behavioral treatment, especially CBT-I, remains first-line in major guidelines. Medication choice is individualized.

### Why use generic names in the article?

Generic names are stable across markets and research. Brand names are used once for search recognition, then the article uses the generic name consistently.

## Regional and formulation note

Approval status, labels, and clinical practice vary by country. Verify the exact product label, manufacturer, strength, release system, and local clinical pathway before publication or use. A tablet or capsule instruction that is correct for one product may be wrong for another.
