# One Drug at a Time: Evidence and Logic in Polypharmacy Deprescribing

> **Editorial status:** Evidence-based editorial draft. Independent clinical and pharmacy review is required before publication. Evidence cutoff: **2026-08-18**. This page is educational and does not provide an individualized dose schedule.

## Key answer

Changing one medication at a time is a strong practical default for attribution and safety, but it is based more on clinical logic and implementation principles than on universal randomized evidence. The practical interpretation must remain within the population, duration, formulation, and outcomes that were actually studied. [1–4]

A safe interpretation starts with the exact medication or intervention, the reason it is being used, the formulation, treatment duration, other medicines, current symptoms, and the risks of both continuing and changing treatment. The article therefore explains decision factors and safety boundaries rather than prescribing a fixed schedule.

This draft prioritizes regulator-hosted product information, current clinical guidance, systematic reviews, randomized trials, and carefully labeled observational or mechanistic evidence. The references below are used according to their design and limitations [1–4]. Absence of a detected signal is not rewritten as absence of risk, and a population average is not presented as an individual prediction.

## Working definitions

| Term | Meaning in this article |

## The finding in one sentence

Changing one medication at a time is a strong practical default for attribution and safety, but it is based more on clinical logic and implementation principles than on universal randomized evidence.

## Why this question matters

Polypharmacy users may receive conflicting instructions to reduce several medicines. A simple rule helps, but emergencies, cross-tapers, toxicity, and interacting sedatives can require coordinated simultaneous action.

## What evidence was reviewed

Evidence includes deprescribing guidelines, medication-review trials, pharmacovigilance, and clinical reasoning about causal attribution. Few trials randomize one-at-a-time versus simultaneous tapering across diverse medication combinations.

## What the studies found

Sequential changes make it easier to link symptoms, benefits, and adverse effects to a specific exposure. They reduce regimen complexity and allow learning before the next decision. Simultaneous changes can shorten overall burden but increase uncertainty and may compound withdrawal.

## Key limitations and sources of uncertainty

Direct comparative evidence is sparse. One-at-a-time plans can take years, prolong exposure to a harmful interaction, or fail when medicines were intentionally linked. The highest-risk medication may need urgent action before an ideal observation period.

## What this may change in practice

Use one-at-a-time as a default with explicit exceptions: acute toxicity, dangerous interaction, supervised cross-taper, hospitalization, pregnancy, or another urgent indication. Document which outcome will be observed before the next change.

## What the evidence does not justify

The principle does not justify leaving a life-threatening combination unchanged, nor does it identify which medication should be first. It should not become a rigid app rule.

## What research should do next

Pragmatic polypharmacy trials should compare sequencing strategies, time to benefit, total adverse events, withdrawal, hospital use, patient burden, and interpretability.

## Funding and conflict-of-interest note

Drug-development studies are often sponsored by manufacturers, while guideline panels and independent reviews can also include authors with industry relationships. Publication should list the funding source and declared conflicts for every pivotal study. A declared conflict does not invalidate a result, but it raises the importance of protocol registration, complete outcome reporting, independent replication, and separation of evidence from promotional language.

## When urgent medical assessment may be needed

        Seek urgent local medical assessment when any of the following applies. This list is intentionally conservative and is not a diagnostic checklist:

        - A trial result is described as proof for populations, durations, doses, or combinations that were not studied
- Absence of a detected signal is rewritten as a categorical absence-of-risk claim
- A network meta-analysis is presented as a head-to-head randomized comparison
- Regulatory approval in one country is described as global approval

        Emergency pathways and telephone numbers vary by country. A tracking application or educational article cannot rule out an emergency.

## Questions to take to the prescriber

- Does this evidence apply to my diagnosis, age, treatment duration, formulation, and other medications?
- Was the finding based on a randomized comparison, an indirect comparison, an observational study, or mechanistic modeling?
- Were withdrawal, relapse, function, serious adverse events, and long-term outcomes measured directly?
- What was not studied, and what uncertainty remains for my situation?
- Would this finding actually change a clinical decision, or only support further research?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is one at a time evidence-based?

It is strongly supported by clinical logic and guidelines, but universal randomized evidence is limited.

### When should several drugs change together?

Examples include a supervised cross-taper, acute toxicity, or an urgent dangerous interaction.

### How long should observation last?

It depends on half-life, active metabolites, withdrawal, relapse risk, and the outcome being measured.

### Can supplements be ignored?

No. They can interact and should be included in the sequence.

## Regional and formulation note

Approval status, labels, and clinical practice vary by country. Verify the exact product label, manufacturer, strength, release system, and local clinical pathway before publication or use. A tablet or capsule instruction that is correct for one product may be wrong for another.
